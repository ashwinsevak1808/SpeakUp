Introduction: Speaking Up Without Shouting

Group discussions, or GDs, are common in college admissions, campus placements and team meetings. In a GD, eight to ten people discuss a topic for around fifteen minutes. Many learners think the winner is the loudest person. In fact, the best GD performers are those who listen, add value and connect ideas, not those who talk the most. In this chapter, you will learn how to take part with calm confidence.

In this chapter you will learn:

  • What evaluators look for in a group discussion
  • How to start, agree, disagree and interrupt politely
  • A simple structure for making a point
  • How to deal with dominant and quiet members
  • How to summarise at the end

Lesson 1What Evaluators Look For

Quality over quantity

Three well-made points are better than ten loud ones. Evaluators remember the person who made a sharp point, helped the group, or brought the discussion back on track.

Lesson 2How to Start

If you are comfortable with the topic, starting a GD gives you a strong first impression. Do not rush. A calm, structured start is better than a fast one.

Type of startExample
Define the topic"Let me begin by defining what remote work means. It is…"
Give a fact"Many employees today say they prefer flexible work arrangements."
Pose a question"Is remote work really more productive? Let's look at both sides."
Join later"Building on what Rahul said, I'd like to add that…"

Sample dialogue · A polite disagreement

KaranI think students should not be allowed to use phones in class.

MeeraI see your point, Karan, because phones can distract. However, they can also help students look up information quickly.

KaranThat's true. Maybe a rule that allows phones for learning only?

MeeraExactly. That seems like a good balance.

Lesson 4Make Your Point with P.R.E.P.

When it is your turn, use P.R.E.P. to speak clearly and briefly in about 30 seconds.

  1. Point. State your point. "I believe online classes are useful."
  2. Reason. Give the reason. "They save travel time and money."
  3. Example. Back it up. "For example, a student from a small town can attend a top teacher's class."
  4. Point again. Wrap up. "So, online classes make education more accessible."

Lesson 5Tricky Situations and the Summary

  • A dominant speaker: Wait for a pause, then say, "That's a good point, Rohan. May I add a different angle?"
  • Others are off-topic: "That's interesting, but to come back to our main topic…"
  • You have no idea about the topic: Listen first. Then agree with a point and add a small, logical example.
  • You are silent: Aim to speak 2 or 3 times. Even short, relevant points count.

If time allows, give a short summary at the end: "To sum up, we discussed the benefits and risks of remote work. Most of us agree it works best as a hybrid model." A good summary shows leadership.

Tips and Tricks

GD success habits
  • Read newspapers and watch the news regularly. Content comes from knowledge.
  • Keep a small notebook with facts and examples on common topics: education, technology, environment, economy.
  • Practise in a group of friends once a week and record the session.
  • Listen actively. Make one short note while others speak, so you can refer to their points by name.
  • Stay calm and polite, even when you disagree strongly.

Try This Week · The Friends' GD

Gather three or four friends and hold a 10-minute discussion on a topic like "Is social media good for students?" Ask one person to be the observer. Use at least two polite-disagreement phrases and one P.R.E.P. point. Ask the observer to note who listened, who added value and who summarised. Share feedback kindly.

Chapter Summary

A group discussion rewards the thoughtful speaker, not the loudest one. Start with a clear point, use P.R.E.P., agree and disagree politely, bring in quiet members and summarise at the end. These same skills will make you a better team member in every meeting you attend. In the next chapter, we turn to the interview room.

Key takeaways
  • Evaluators look for content, communication, listening, teamwork and leadership.
  • Start calmly with a definition, fact, question or by building on someone's point.
  • Disagree with "I see it differently", never "You are wrong".
  • Use P.R.E.P.: Point, Reason, Example, Point.
  • A short, fair summary shows leadership.
