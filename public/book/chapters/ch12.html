Introduction: The Fear Everyone Shares

Surveys across the world show that many people fear public speaking more than almost anything else. If your heart races, your hands shake and your mind goes blank when you stand in front of people, you are completely normal. The difference between a confident speaker and a nervous one is rarely talent. It is preparation, structure and a few simple techniques. All of them can be learned.

In this chapter you will learn:

  • Why nerves happen, and how to turn them into energy
  • A simple structure for any speech: Opening, Body, Closing
  • Four ways to start with a strong hook
  • How to prepare and practise without memorising
  • What to do if you forget what to say

Lesson 1Understanding Your Nerves

When you feel nervous, your body releases adrenaline. Your heart beats faster, your breathing gets shallow and your hands feel cold. This is the same reaction you feel before a cricket match or an exam. It is your body getting ready to perform.

Reframe it

Instead of telling yourself "I'm scared", say "I'm excited. My body is getting ready." Research suggests that this small change helps people perform better than trying to calm down.

Lesson 2The Three-Part Structure

A speech without structure is like a journey without a map. Use this simple frame for any talk, long or short.

  1. Opening (10%). Get attention, say who you are and what the talk is about.
  2. Body (80%). Make two or three main points. Support each with a short example or story. The "rule of three" is easy for the audience to remember.
  3. Closing (10%). Summarise the key message and end with a clear action, thought or thank-you.

Lesson 3Four Strong Openings

HookExample
A question"How many of you have ever been afraid to speak in front of others?"
A short story"Last year, I froze in front of 200 people. Today, I want to tell you what I learned."
A surprising fact"Most people spend more time choosing a phone than preparing for a job interview."
A bold statement"Confidence is not something you are born with. It is something you practise."

After the hook, introduce yourself briefly: "Good morning, everyone. My name is Pooja, and today I'll talk about three habits that improved my English." Notice: My name is Pooja, not "Myself Pooja".

Lesson 4Prepare Without Memorising

Memorising a full script is risky. If you forget one line, you may lose everything. Instead, prepare an outline and practise speaking it aloud.

  1. Write your key message in one sentence. "Small daily practice beats big weekend efforts."
  2. List 3 main points on a small card, in short keywords.
  3. Add one example for each point from your own life.
  4. Practise aloud three times. Say it differently each time. Time yourself.
  5. Practise once in front of someone. A friend, a family member or a mirror.

Tips and Tricks

Speak with calm confidence
  • Arrive early. Walk around the stage or room and check the equipment.
  • Take three slow breaths before you begin: in for 4, out for 6.
  • Start with a smile and one friendly face in the audience.
  • Speak to one person at a time, not to "the crowd".
  • Never apologise for being nervous. Most of the time, nobody notices.
  • Finish strong, and pause a second before saying "Thank you".

Try This Week · The Two-Minute Talk

Choose a topic you love. Write a 3-keyword outline (opening hook, two points, closing line). Speak for two minutes, three times: alone, in front of a mirror, and in front of one person. Record the last one. Watch it and note what you did well and one thing to change.

Chapter Summary

Nervousness is natural, and it can be turned into energy. A simple three-part structure, a strong hook, an outline instead of a script and a few rescue phrases are all you need to speak with calm confidence. Next, you will use these skills to build presentations that inform and persuade.

Key takeaways
  • Nerves are energy. Reframe "I'm scared" to "I'm excited".
  • Use Opening, Body (2 or 3 points), Closing.
  • Start with a question, story, fact or bold statement.
  • Use an outline, not a memorised script, and practise aloud.
  • If you blank out: pause, breathe, and use a rescue phrase.
