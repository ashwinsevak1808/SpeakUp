Introduction: What You Say Is Only Half the Message

Imagine two people saying the same sentence: "I'm very happy to be here." The first speaks quietly, looks at the floor and crosses her arms. The second smiles, stands tall and speaks warmly. Which one do you believe? Almost always, the second. Communication is more than words. Your posture, face, hands and voice send a second message, and when it does not match your words, people believe the body.

In this chapter you will learn:

  • Eye contact, posture and gestures that build trust
  • The five parts of your voice: pace, pitch, volume, pause and tone
  • How stress changes the meaning of a sentence
  • How to control nervous habits
  • A daily five-minute routine to improve both

Lesson 1Body Language That Builds Trust

PartWhat worksWhat to avoid
Eye contactLook at the person's face for 3 to 5 seconds, then glance away naturally. With a group, share your eyes across the room.Staring without a break, or always looking at the floor, ceiling or notes.
PostureStand or sit tall, shoulders relaxed, feet flat and slightly apart.Slouching, leaning on the table, crossing arms tightly.
HandsUse open, natural gestures at waist level to support your words.Hiding hands in pockets, fidgeting with a pen, pointing a finger.
FaceA relaxed, genuine smile and an expression that matches your message.A blank or frozen face, or a smile at serious moments.
HandshakeFirm but not crushing, with a smile and eye contact. Let go after two or three shakes.A limp handshake or holding on too long.
A note on culture

In India, lowering your eyes can show respect to elders. In most international business settings, steady eye contact shows confidence and honesty. Learn to adjust: respectful in one situation, direct in another.

Lesson 2The Five Parts of Your Voice

Pacehow fast you speakPitchhow high or lowVolumehow loudPausethe silent momentsTonethe feeling behind words
  • Pace: Aim for about 120 to 150 words a minute. Nervous speakers rush, so slow down on important points.
  • Pitch: A flat voice sounds bored. Let your pitch go up on questions and exciting words, and down at the end of a statement.
  • Volume: Speak so that the back of the room can hear you without strain. Not too soft, not shouting.
  • Pause: A short pause before and after a key point makes it powerful. Silence is not an error.
  • Tone: Choose a tone that fits: warm for friends, calm for customers, firm for rules.

Lesson 3Stress Changes the Meaning

In English, the word you stress tells the listener what is important. Read the sentence below five times, stressing a different word each time. The meaning changes every time.

Stressed wordWhat it suggests
I didn't take your pen.Someone else did.
I didn't take your pen.It is not true that I did.
I didn't take your pen.I borrowed or moved it, but did not steal it.
I didn't take your pen.I took someone else's pen.
I didn't take your pen.I took something else of yours.

Lesson 4Taming Nervous Habits

Nerves show in small ways: hands that shake, words that rush, "umm" and "you know". You cannot remove nerves, but you can manage them.

  1. Breathe low. Before you speak, breathe in slowly through your nose for 4 counts, hold for 2, breathe out for 6.
  2. Plant your feet. Standing firmly sends a message to your brain that you are stable.
  3. Pause instead of "umm". A silent pause sounds thoughtful. A filler sounds unsure.
  4. Give your hands a job. Hold a pen, a clicker or the lectern lightly, or use natural gestures.

Common Mistakes and Corrections

Wrong habitBetter habit
Speaking very fast when nervous.Breathe, slow down, and pause after each idea.
Looking at the floor or notes all the time.Look at faces for a few seconds, then at your notes.
Speaking in a flat, monotone voice.Vary your pitch and stress the key words.
Saying "umm", "like" and "you know" often.Use a silent pause or a short phrase like "Let me think."
Hands in pockets or arms crossed.Hands relaxed, using open gestures.

Tips and Tricks

Build your presence
  • Record a short video of yourself speaking. Watch it once with sound and once without sound: what does your body say?
  • Practise in front of a mirror for two minutes a day.
  • Warm up your voice with humming and slow tongue twisters before speaking.
  • Drink water at room temperature. Avoid very cold drinks before a talk.
  • Choose one habit to improve each week. Do not try to change everything at once.

Chapter Summary

Your body and voice speak before and with your words. Steady eye contact, an open posture, natural gestures, a varied voice and well-placed pauses make you look and sound confident, even when you feel nervous. Part 3 is complete: you can now chat, call, listen and present yourself with presence. In Part 4, you will use all of it for college, career and leadership, starting with public speaking.

Key takeaways
  • People believe your body and voice more than your words when they do not match.
  • Use steady eye contact, an open posture and natural hand gestures.
  • Control pace, pitch, volume, pause and tone.
  • Stress changes meaning, so stress the words that matter.
  • Replace nervous habits with breathing and pauses.
