Introduction: Be Understood First

You do not need a British or American accent. You need to be easily understood. An Indian accent is perfectly fine. What matters is a small set of sounds and stresses that can change the meaning of a word. Fix those, and people will understand you the first time.

In this chapter you will learn:

  • The six sounds that cause most confusion for Indian speakers of English
  • Word stress: the most powerful pronunciation skill
  • How to say the endings -ed and -s correctly
  • Common silent letters
  • A 10-minute daily routine with tongue twisters

Lesson 1Accent vs Clarity

An accent is the "music" of your speech. Clarity is whether the listener understands you. You can keep your accent and still be perfectly clear. Our goal is clarity, not copying someone else's voice.

Remember

Your mother tongue is not a problem. It is simply the reason some English sounds feel new to your mouth. New sounds need new muscle habits, and habits can be built in a few weeks.

Lesson 2Six Tricky Sounds

1. V and W

Many learners use one sound for both. They are different. For V, your top teeth touch your lower lip. For W, your lips make a small round "oo" shape and the teeth do not touch.

V (teeth on lip)W (round lips)
vestwest
vinewine
verywary
veilwhale

2. TH: two sounds

Put the tip of your tongue lightly between your teeth. In think, three, bath blow soft air with no voice. In this, that, mother add your voice, so your throat buzzes. Do not replace it with a hard "t" or "d" (not "tink" or "dis").

3. Short and long vowels

Length can change the word completely.

ShortLong
shipsheep
liveleave
fullfool
pullpool

4. T and D: touch the ridge, not the roof

In many Indian languages, "t" and "d" are made with the tongue curled back. In English, the tongue tip touches the small bump just behind your upper front teeth and moves away quickly, with a lighter touch. Try: today, ten, daily, department.

5. Words that start with S + a letter

Words like school, station, speak, student are sometimes said with an extra vowel at the front ("iskool", "istation"). Start with a long, calm "sss" and then add the rest: sss-chool, sss-tation, sss-peak.

6. The Z sound

The Z in zoo, zebra, lazy buzzes in your throat, like a bee. It is not the same as "j". Place your fingers on your throat and feel the buzz.

Say it in Hinglish first, then in English: Main school jaata hoon. → I go to school. (Remember: sss-chool.)

Lesson 3Word Stress: The Secret of Clear English

English words have one strong syllable. Say it louder, longer and clearer. Putting the stress on the wrong syllable is the biggest reason people say "sorry, can you repeat?"

Same word, different job

Some words change stress when they change from noun to verb.

How to find the stress
  • Check the word in an online dictionary and listen to the audio. The stress mark (ˈ) comes just before the strong syllable.
  • Tap the table once per syllable. The beat that feels "biggest" is your stress.

Lesson 4The Endings: -ed and -s

The past-tense ending -ed has three sounds, and the spelling does not tell you which.

Ending soundWhenExamples
/t/after sounds like p, k, s, ch, shworked, stopped, watched, missed
/d/after most other soundsplayed, cleaned, called, lived
/id/ (extra syllable)after t or dwanted, needed, decided, started

The same idea works for -s: cats (s), dogs (z), buses (iz). Do not worry about being perfect. Just make sure you pronounce the ending, because it tells the listener about time and number.

Sample Dialogue

Sample dialogue · At a clinic reception

ReceptionistGood morning. How can I help you?

MeeraGood morning. I have an appointment at ten o'clock with Doctor Joshi.

ReceptionistSorry, did you say ten or tin?

MeeraTen o'clock, please. T-E-N.

ReceptionistThank you. Please wait in the waiting room. The doctor will see you shortly.

Notice how a small vowel difference (ten and tin) can change the message. This is why clear vowels matter.

Tips and Tricks

Practise like a speaker
  • Record yourself on your phone. Your ears will notice what your mouth cannot feel.
  • Use a mirror for TH and V/W: you can see teeth and tongue.
  • Slow is smooth, smooth is fast. Say tricky words slowly first, then speed up.
  • Learn new words with their audio, never only from the spelling.
  • Practise 10 minutes every day rather than one hour on Sunday.

Tongue twisters for your daily 10 minutes

V/W: "Very well, very well, we were very well."   TH: "Three thin thieves thought they thrilled the throne."   S: "She sells sea shells by the seashore."   R/L: "Red lorry, yellow lorry." Start slowly and speed up only when every sound is clear.

Try This Week · Your Sound Diary

Each day, choose one tricky sound from Lesson 2. Say its example words 10 times, then record yourself reading a short paragraph that contains many of them. Listen once. Smile at what improved, and note one thing to fix tomorrow. By Sunday you will have recorded seven small proofs of progress.

Chapter Summary

Good pronunciation is not about sounding foreign. It is about being clear. Six sounds, word stress and the endings cover most of what matters. Practise a little every day with audio, not just spelling, and your confidence will grow with your clarity. Next, you will build the words to use all these sounds on.

Key takeaways
  • Aim for clarity, not a foreign accent.
  • Fix V/W, TH, short/long vowels, T/D, S-clusters and Z first.
  • Word stress matters more than single sounds. Learn it with each new word.
  • Say the endings -ed and -s so listeners know the time and number.
  • Listen to audio, record yourself and practise 10 minutes daily.
